THE PUBLIC RECORD · FINDINGS

Findings

Before anything is published here it is investigated: a question is asked of a source, the source is measured, and a decision is written down. This page collects those investigations — what was tested, what the measurement showed, and what was decided. Each links to its full document in the repository.

Roughly half the decisions are refusals. That is not a claim about concealment. Some sources are good and simply cannot be linked to anything else; some are complete but measure a different kind of quantity than the rest of the record; and several refusals are the Ledger's own editorial judgement about what a reader would predictably misread. Which of those applies is stated in each entry, because they are different reasons and a reader may disagree with any of them on the evidence shown.

The three decisions

Every investigation ends in one of three places. The vocabulary is the same one the data pages use, so a tier named here is the tier printed on the record.

WHAT A DECISION MEANS Gatedevery entity-year reproduces a total the source itself published, to a named resolution. If it does not reproduce, nothing is written and the previous data stays. A gated layer names its reconciliation target. As filedpublished with a visible label saying no published control exists to check it against. The figures are the government's own; nobody has confirmed them, and the page says so on every record rather than in a method note. Refusednot published, with the measurement that decided it. A refusal is a finding, not an absence of work — and it is recorded so it can be revisited when a source changes.

A fourth state appears inside otherwise-shipped layers: a held year or figure, where the source exists but cannot be reconciled, shown as a labelled absence rather than published at a lower standard.

Refused

These are the questions the Ledger was asked, or asked itself, and answered no. The measurement is given rather than summarised, because the measurement is the argument.

Vendor and contract search — who the state pays REFUSED
INVESTIGATEDWhether a reader could ask what a named company received from the state, using the state's own vendor-payment files.
MEASUREDIn the most recent published month, payments carrying a vendor name totalled $4.807B against $40.897B of recorded spending — 11.8%. Names are free text truncated at about thirty characters, so one company resolves to several strings: AT&T appears as 8 distinct names.
DECIDEDDon't ship. A per-vendor total built on this would be an unlabelled floor — roughly a tenth of the universe, with the remainder distributed unknowably per vendor. A later pass corrected this finding's phrasing that several large departments were “absent entirely”: they are present thinly, which is worse for a search product than clean absence, because a sliver looks like an answer.

Recipient data — a second pass at every route REFUSED AT STATE LEVEL
INVESTIGATEDSeven routes to recipient, payee or vendor data, each tested against a live endpoint and given its own tier — including whether a complete slice for particular departments could be gated even if the whole could not.
MEASUREDVendor-named payments $4,806,857,530 across 315,191 rows and 14,872 distinct names; the same month's full spending file $40,897,014,501 across 3,930,969 rows — 11.75% of recorded spending carries a vendor name. The subset test fails: per-department vendor-named dollars range from 0.7% of that department's spending to over 180%, and a ratio above 100% is impossible if one were a subset of the other.
DECIDEDMixed, and worth not flattening. Four routes not buildable; the state grants portal and the federal spending record as-filed only, the latter as an explicitly federal record; and one route gated — the San Francisco and Los Angeles check registers, each reconciling to its own city's audited statements. Nothing at the state level clears the gate. The one place a reconciled recipient layer is possible is local, and the entry below is what happened when that was tested.

The Los Angeles checkbook — a recipient index where the data is good REFUSED
INVESTIGATEDWhether one city's unusually complete checkbook — 6,384,402 rows with a vendor identifier on all but two — could carry a recipient index the statewide files could not.
MEASUREDThe city's own privacy mask covers 472,993 rows, and has a hole exactly where the expectation of privacy is strongest: of 18,526 rows flagged as a settlement or judgment, only 622 fall under a privacy token. The remainder carry real payee names — 9,370 distinct, unredacted, with a redaction-token scan matching approximately zero.
DECIDEDDon't build it. This refusal is the Ledger's own judgement rather than a limit of the source: the data is strong, and building an index over it would republish, sorted and searchable, the names of people who sued the city and settled.

Debt and long-term obligations REFUSED
INVESTIGATEDFive sources — CDIAC, the State Controller's city, county and district filings, state general-obligation debt, the two big pension systems, and K-12 and community-college bonds — against the question of whether a debt figure can sit on a page of annual spending.
MEASUREDCalifornia cities filed $114,363,062,810 of outstanding debt for FY2024 against published city expenditures of $115,830,469,221 in the same year. The stock is 0.99× the flow — numerically indistinguishable on a page whose entire vocabulary is annual spending.
DECIDEDShip nothing, on all five sources. On three of them the data is better than expected and one gate reproduces a published control exactly. The refusal is a design verdict about stock and flow, not a data-quality verdict.

Reserves and fund balances REFUSED
INVESTIGATEDWhether a reserve figure — a total, a five-way GASB 54 breakdown, an unassigned-only figure, or a months-of-spending ratio — could be published for cities, counties, special districts, K-12 or the state.
MEASUREDThe classification is complete on three layers and the filers' arithmetic is perfect. No published control total exists on any local layer. Unassigned balance is 16.3% of the total, with committed at 7.7% and nonspendable at 0.4% — so an unassigned-only figure would describe a sixth of the thing it appears to name.
DECIDEDShip nothing, and no separate reserves page. One thing does ship: a refusal sentence on the layer pages, in the same dress as the existing per-resident and no-totals blocks.
DOCUMENTdocs/V24_RESERVES_FINDING.md
Negative fund balances — the narrow case reserves left open PER-RECORD STATEMENT ONLY
INVESTIGATEDWhether the bare fact that an entity filed a negative total governmental fund balance could be published, where a reserve figure could not.
MEASUREDAcross eight fiscal years, 294 entities carry a negative total at least once. 132 of them — 45% — are negative in exactly one year of eight; only 11 are negative in all eight. The steady annual count (87, 89, 83, 95, 93, 89, 89, 95) conceals a largely rotating population.
DECIDEDNo layer, no list, no count, no dollar figure. A neutral per-record statement on the entity's own record, carrying the count of years, because that count is the only thing in the data separating a one-year event from an eight-year condition.
DOCUMENTdocs/V24A_NEGATIVE_BALANCE_FINDING.md
Voter-approved bonds against what is spent from them REFUSED
INVESTIGATEDWhether an identifier chain survives from a ballot measure, to the bond issuance it authorised, to the fund the money is spent from.
MEASUREDThe chain breaks three times. 0 of 273 election-record district names match a bond-issuance issuer name exactly. Only 177 of 20,253 K-12 issues — 0.87% — name a ballot measure at all, in free prose. And the school accounting has no measure dimension: 84.6% of $8,740,953,847 of bond-fund spending sits in a single object code while ballots name a median of four purposes.
DECIDEDDon't ship. Closing any of the three links would require the Ledger's own judgement about which spending serves which measure, which is editorial. Placing a ballot purpose beside a spending figure implies they reconcile; they do not.
DOCUMENTdocs/V25_MEASURES_FINDING.md
Adoption provenance — who approved a budget, and when REFUSED
INVESTIGATEDWhether each published figure could carry the body that adopted it and the date, from the sources the site already reads.
MEASUREDOnly one layer's own figures were adopted by anybody; the rest are year-end accounting records, which nobody adopts. Where the date does exist it barely varies: nine Budget Acts signed on June 26, 27 or 28, six of nine on June 27. Across 27,916 State Controller column names there is no adoption date at all.
DECIDEDDon't ship — the field would be decorative. The site already carries the version that works, on the one layer whose provenance genuinely varies: community-college apportionments are stamped with their round, because those stages produce different numbers.
DOCUMENTdocs/V26_ADOPTION_FINDING.md
Historical depth — how far back each layer honestly reaches PARTLY REFUSED
INVESTIGATEDWhether the record could answer “how has this moved over decades” rather than over a handful of years. Every local-government source reaches back twenty-two years, so availability was almost never the constraint.
MEASUREDYes for four layers, no for two, partly for three. Cities and counties stay at FY2016-17: twenty-two years are available and the totals reconcile, but the function split these pages are built on does not exist before then, and six of eight functions change meaning at the boundary. CSU cannot be extended at all — the source returns HTTP 403 to every scripted request, so an older year's control total is uncomputable rather than merely unreconciled.
DECIDEDExtend where the older data still means the same thing; refuse where it does not. A year that cannot be gated does not ship.

Shipped, and at what tier

Each layer on the site earned a tier by measurement, not by category. Where a gate reproduces a published control the layer is gated and the resolution is named at the source's own precision; where no control exists the layer is published as filed and says so on every record.

K-12 school districts GATED · TO THE CENT
INVESTIGATEDWhether district general-ledger filings could be compared per pupil against a control the Department of Education itself publishes.
DECIDEDShip gated and comparable, with the comparison scoped to school districts — county offices and charters are published as records only and never enter the per-pupil statistic, matching the source's own exclusion.
RECONCILES TOCDE's published Current Expense of Education.

Revenue, layer by layer MIXED TIERS · PER LAYER
INVESTIGATEDWhether the record could show where money comes from as well as where it goes, and whether one tier could cover every layer.
DECIDEDShip revenue layer by layer, at the tier each layer earns rather than at one blended tier. No surplus or deficit is computed anywhere: revenue and spending are both flows but are measured on different scopes, so subtracting them would produce a number the source does not support.

DOCUMENTdocs/V21_REVENUE_FINDING.md
Public employee compensation AS FILED · WITH CONDITIONS
INVESTIGATEDFirst whether position-level compensation should be published at all, then — the decision having been reopened — whether it could be published accurately.
MEASUREDA published control exists and can never be reconciled to: the export is a live database with no as-of-date total, so there is no instant at which it and the announced figure can be aligned. A quarter of city rows show pay below half the position's own stated minimum, and the source carries no hours field, so no average over the population would mean anything.
DECIDEDShip as filed, or not at all. The earlier decision refused this layer on privacy grounds; that refusal was withdrawn, and the reason is recorded rather than left implicit. The conditions are not optional: no average or median, no ranking, no share of spending, and a mark on every employer whose pension reporting is not comparable.

Reading these against the record

Each entry above states a measurement and a decision. The two are separable on purpose: a reader can accept every figure and still think a decision went the wrong way, and the documents are written so that argument can be had on the evidence rather than on trust. Where a decision was the Ledger's own judgement rather than a limit of the data — the Los Angeles checkbook, the ratio refusals, the compensation reversal — the entry says so, because that is the kind of decision most worth disputing.

The findings are also a record of the project's own errors. Investigations have been reopened, measurements retracted after a defect was found in the measuring code, and a published claim corrected in seventy-four places when a gate proved it wrong. Those reversals are recorded in the documents rather than edited away. For the method underneath all of it — every source, every gate, and what the site refuses to do — see about & method; for a plain-language guide to the record itself, see reading the Ledger. Every layer described here is downloadable in full as bulk data, one CSV per layer with the tier and basis in the file's own header.
